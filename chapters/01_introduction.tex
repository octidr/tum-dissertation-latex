\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}

The automotive industry is undergoing a fundamental transformation driven by the
growing role of software and electronics in vehicle design and innovation. Industry
analyses consistently report that the majority of automotive innovation today originates
in electronic systems rather than in mechanical or materials engineering~\cite{ey1},
and that spending on automotive software and electronics is projected to account for
nearly half of the total vehicle bill of materials in premium vehicles by the end of
this decade~\cite{pwc1}. Vehicle electrification, autonomous driving, advanced driver
assistance systems~(ADAS), and vehicle-to-everything~(V2X) connectivity are only some
of the domains where onboard computing has become central to product differentiation
and safety.

A modern high-end vehicle contains upward of 100 Electronic Control Units~(ECUs)
connected by several kilometers of wiring harness, contributing significantly to
vehicle weight and manufacturing cost~\cite{vipin2,baunach1}. Historically, ECUs
have been designed with a single-purpose philosophy: each unit is dedicated to one
functional domain in order to avoid interference between tasks and to simplify safety
certification~\cite{vipin1,vipin2}. While this approach provides clear isolation and
predictable behavior, it has well-known structural shortcomings. Many tasks are only
active during specific and rare operating conditions, leading to chronic
underutilization of processing resources across the fleet. A failure of any single ECU
with no fallback mechanism can also compromise the functions it hosts. The combined
effect is a system that is simultaneously over-provisioned in hardware and fragile in
fault tolerance.

These inefficiencies have motivated a broader industry transition toward \emph{ECU
	consolidation}: redistributing workloads from many single-purpose units to a smaller
number of high-performance, multi-purpose computing platforms~\cite{mckinsey1}. The
emerging software-defined vehicle~(SDV) concept extends this further, treating vehicle
functions as software services that can be deployed, updated, and redistributed across
a flexible hardware architecture at runtime~\cite{sdv2024}. Under this paradigm, the
static binding between a function and a specific ECU is relaxed, and the ability to
dynamically distribute and redistribute tasks at runtime becomes a key enabler of both
resource efficiency and fault tolerance.

Achieving this vision, however, requires solving a problem that sits at the
intersection of two fields that have historically developed largely independently:
\emph{mixed-criticality systems} and \emph{real-time task migration}. This tension
is the central intellectual challenge of this dissertation, and it is worth stating
explicitly from the outset.

Mixed-criticality~(MC) systems research is fundamentally concerned with
\emph{predictability and static assurance}: given a set of tasks with heterogeneous
safety requirements, expressed through standards such as ISO~26262 Automotive Safety
Integrity Levels~(ASILs)~\cite{iso26262_2018}, how can a platform guarantee that
high-criticality tasks always meet their deadlines, even under worst-case conditions,
while also providing useful service to lower-criticality workloads? The entire
analytical apparatus of mixed-criticality scheduling, from Vestal's foundational
model~\cite{vestal2007} to more recent adaptive approaches~\cite{burns2017}, is built
on the assumption that task parameters are known and fixed at design time, and that
the platform topology does not change during execution.

Dynamic task migration, by contrast, is inherently disruptive to this assumption.
Migration transfers an executing task from one node to another, necessarily
interrupting its execution, serializing its state, transmitting that state over a
network, and resuming execution in a different environment. Each of these steps
introduces latency, consumes resources on both the source and destination node, and
temporarily violates the static assumptions on which schedulability guarantees are
built. In a system with hard real-time constraints, where a braking control
function failing its deadline can have fatal consequences, this disruption must be
managed with extreme care.

Reconciling these two requirements is non-trivial. It is not sufficient to apply
existing MC scheduling on each node independently and treat migration as a separate
layer: the act of migration changes the task set on each node, triggering mode
transitions, modifying resource availability, and potentially invalidating
schedulability guarantees that were valid before the migration event. Conversely,
existing real-time migration approaches do not account for criticality-differentiated
treatment of tasks, providing no mechanism to prioritize the migration of safety-critical
functions over best-effort ones when resources are constrained. The intersection of
these two fields, particularly in the context of ECU consolidation for automotive
systems, represents a largely unexplored area in the published literature, and
constitutes the primary motivation for this dissertation.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem}

Prior research at the Chair of Operating Systems of the Technical University of
Munich~(TUM), conducted within projects including KIA4SM, MaLSAMi, and HaCRoM, has
established foundational approaches to task migration in real-time distributed systems.
This includes checkpoint-based migration execution mechanisms and machine-learning-based
migration planning tools capable of runtime schedulability prediction~\cite{taieb1,
	utz1,blieninger1}. Some of these contributions form the direct technical basis on which this
dissertation builds. However, they share some critical limitations: they operate at a
single, undifferentiated criticality level. All tasks are treated equivalently with
respect to scheduling priority and migration policy, and no mechanism exists to
guarantee that high-criticality tasks are privileged during resource contention or
migration events. Furthermore, so far no actual migration mechanism has been implemented that is compatible with execution on a real-time operating system with minimal overhead and fulfilling criticality constraints. 

These limitations becoms untenable in the context of ECU consolidation for
safety-critical automotive systems. When an ASIL-D braking function and a
non-safety-relevant navigation application share the same computing platform and
the same migration infrastructure, they must be treated fundamentally differently.
ISO~26262 mandates that the behavior of safety-critical functions be guaranteed and
verifiable under all relevant operating conditions~\cite{iso26262_2018}; a migration
mechanism that might delay or interrupt such a function in the same way as a
low-criticality task is not compliant with this requirement.

Four concrete open problems define the scope of this dissertation. First, the base
execution platform must be extended to support dynamic loading of executables with a corresponding checkpoint-restore mechanism. Second, the execution platform must also be extended to support mixed-criticality scheduling, providing
the runtime separation of concerns required for correct MC behavior before any migration
mechanism is layered on top. Third, differentiated migration strategies must be
designed for tasks at different criticality levels, each with appropriate timing
guarantees, state preservation mechanisms, and resource overhead characteristics.
The fundamental challenge here is that high-criticality migration must be fast,
bounded, and formally analyzable, while low-criticality migration may trade immediacy
for efficiency. Fourth, the interaction between migration events and the MC scheduler
on each node must be carefully accounted for: a task arriving at a destination ECU
mid-execution has different schedulability implications than a task present since
system startup, and the platform design must handle this correctly.

A secondary problem concerns the role of edge computing resources in this architecture.
Vehicular edge cloud~(VEC) and Mobile Edge Computing~(MEC) infrastructures place
compute capacity in proximity to the vehicle, offering supplementary processing
resources with latencies that may be acceptable for non-safety-critical workloads.
Whether and how such resources can be incorporated as a third tier in a
criticality-stratified migration hierarchy, and what formal conditions bound their
safe use, is an exploratory research question this dissertation investigates.

\section{Research Questions}
\label{sec:researchquestions}

Based on the problem statement above, this dissertation is guided by the following
research questions:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item[\textbf{RQ1.}] To what extent is task migration feasible in a distributed
	real-time embedded system while respecting real-time constraints, and under what
	conditions can timing guarantees be preserved across a migration event?
	
	\item[\textbf{RQ2.}] How should tasks be prioritized and scheduled based on their
	criticality level in a system that must adapt dynamically to hardware failures and
	varying task load, and how can mixed-criticality scheduling be integrated with a
	runtime migration execution layer?
	
	\item[\textbf{RQ3.}] What migration strategies, at both the operating system and
	communication levels, are most suitable for high- and low-criticality tasks,
	considering the trade-off between minimizing migration latency and optimizing
	resource utilization?
	
	\item[\textbf{RQ4.}] Under what conditions is it feasible to offload tasks to
	virtual edge resources as part of a criticality-stratified migration hierarchy,
	and what are the implications with respect to latency, schedulability, safety
	assurance, and resource efficiency?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Research Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

This dissertation follows a design-science research methodology, proceeding through
three stages: system design, prototypical implementation on real hardware, and
empirical evaluation.

The first stage is \emph{system design}. The architecture of the mixed-criticality
base platform, the guest task model, and the differentiated migration strategies are
developed through iterative design informed by the requirements of the target domain.
Design decisions are driven by the constraints of embedded ECU-class hardware (limited
memory, deterministic scheduling requirements, and the isolation demands imposed by
ISO~26262) rather than by theoretical optimality criteria alone.

The second stage is \emph{prototypical implementation}. The designed platform is
realized as a working system on embedded hardware, integrating the Axcan OS
mixed-criticality scheduler, an ELF-based guest task loading mechanism, checkpoint
and state transfer support, and a communication infrastructure based on PTP time
synchronization and SOME/IP. This prototype serves as the primary artifact through
which the design is evaluated.

The third stage is \emph{empirical evaluation}. The implemented system is evaluated
through proof-of-concept demonstrations and direct measurement on the target hardware.
Initial results characterize the task loading latency achievable on embedded nodes
and on virtualized edge nodes respectively, establishing an empirical baseline for
assessing migration feasibility across criticality tiers. Further timing and
schedulability measurements are planned as the platform matures. The emphasis
throughout is on grounding claims in observed system behavior on real hardware,
rather than in simulation or purely analytical bounds.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

This dissertation makes the following original contributions to the state of the art
in mixed-criticality real-time systems and distributed embedded computing:

\begin{enumerate}
	
	\item \textbf{Mixed-criticality integration into a distributed real-time embedded
		platform.} The FreeRTOS-based ESFREE platform is extended to support
	mixed-criticality scheduling with an Asymmetric Multiprocessing~(AMP) execution
	model on multicore ECU hardware. This provides the foundational runtime separation
	of criticality levels required for all subsequent contributions.
	
	\item \textbf{Guest task architecture and ELF-based migration support.} A portable
	guest task architecture is defined in the form of a C library and a simplified
	linker script, enabling tasks to be compiled as self-describing ELF binaries that
	can be loaded and relocated at runtime on a target ECU. The architecture integrates
	checkpoint support and PTP-synchronized timing to preserve task progress and timing
	context across migration events.
	
	\item \textbf{Criticality-differentiated migration strategies.} Three migration
	strategies are defined and implemented, each targeting a distinct criticality tier.
	The high-criticality~(HI) strategy provides a fast, formally bounded migration path
	with timing guarantees sufficient for ASIL-class tasks. The low-criticality~(LO)
	strategy employs a checkpoint-restore mechanism that prioritizes continuity of
	progress while minimizing interference with co-hosted high-criticality tasks. The
	Virtual ECU strategy defines the conditions and mechanisms under which
	non-safety-critical tasks may be offloaded to edge computing resources, and
	characterizes the associated latency and reliability constraints.
	
	\item \textbf{Empirical characterization of task loading latency across execution
		tiers.} Initial measurements on the target hardware establish the task loading
	latency achievable by the Axcan OS ELF loading mechanism on embedded nodes, and
	the additional overhead introduced by containerization on virtualized edge nodes.
	These results provide a concrete empirical baseline for assessing the feasibility
	and overhead of migration across the different criticality tiers, and motivate the
	design choices made in the differentiated migration strategies.
	
	\item \textbf{Mixed-criticality-aware adaptation of an ML-based migration planning
		toolchain.} The machine-learning-based schedulability prediction tools developed
	in prior theses~\cite{taieb1,utz1,blieninger1} are adapted to embedded ECU-class
	hardware and extended to incorporate mixed-criticality task parameters, enabling
	MC-aware runtime migration planning.
	
\end{enumerate}
